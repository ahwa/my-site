# Global Market Context — Q1 2026

**Date:** March 28, 2026
**Prepared for:** Investment Committee Review

---

## Executive Summary

- **Stagflation risk is the dominant macro theme.** The US economy decelerated sharply in Q4 2025 (GDP +0.7% annualized) while core PCE re-accelerated to 3.1% YoY in January 2026. The Fed is caught between elevated inflation and a weakening labor market (Feb payrolls: -92K, unemployment: 4.4%).
- **US equity valuations remain stretched despite a -7% YTD drawdown.** The S&P 500 sits at 6,369 (down from its January 2026 ATH of 7,003), with a Shiller CAPE of 36.7x — more than double the historical median of 16x — leaving limited margin of safety against earnings or rate shocks.
- **A live US-Israel-Iran military conflict is the defining geopolitical shock of Q1 2026.** WTI crude surged to ~$100/barrel (+44% YoY), Qatar's LNG capacity is disrupted, and the Strait of Hormuz is at risk of closure — a 1970s-style energy shock scenario is now non-trivial.
- **Gold and real assets are the standout performers.** Gold hit an all-time high of $5,608/oz in January 2026 and is currently at ~$4,495/oz (+46% YoY), driven by dollar weakness, geopolitical risk, and central bank buying; the DXY has weakened to ~100, with EUR/USD at 1.15.
- **Positioning implication:** Reduce duration and US large-cap equity beta; overweight commodities/real assets and international value (UK, Japan); maintain dry powder for H2 entry points as geopolitical clarity emerges. Credit spreads and Bitcoin (at $66K) reflect insufficient fear relative to macro risks.

## Geopolitics

### US–Iran/Middle East Conflict (Most Acute Risk)

The defining geopolitical event of Q1 2026 is the active US-Israel military campaign against Iran, which began in late February 2026 and entered its second month as of late March. This is not a proxy conflict — US forces are directly engaged. Key developments:

- Iran's South Pars gas field has sustained significant damage, disrupting a major share of global LNG supply.
- Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG facility — responsible for ~30% of global LNG trade — has suffered damage, prompting QatarEnergy to declare force majeure on LNG supply contracts. This effectively eliminates roughly 17% of Qatar's LNG export capacity for up to five years.
- Yemen's Houthis have entered the conflict, pledging additional attacks on Israel and regional infrastructure.
- Iran has threatened retaliatory strikes on US and Israeli targets across the region. The Strait of Hormuz — through which ~20% of global energy trade transits — is at active risk of disruption.
- Oil at ~$100/barrel reflects significant risk premium but not yet a full Hormuz-closure scenario ($130–160/barrel in that case). Brent is at ~$104/barrel.
- The IEA has warned this could represent the most severe energy shock since the 1970s if it escalates further.
- Preliminary US-Iran diplomatic back-channel discussions were reportedly beginning in late March, providing some geopolitical hope that tempered copper and oil prices modestly.

### US–China Relations

The Trump 2.0 administration has maintained and intensified the trade conflict with China inherited from Trump 1.0 and continued (in modified form) by Biden:

- Tariff baseline on Chinese goods: approximately 60% across most categories, with specific sectors (EVs, semiconductors, solar) facing higher rates (100%+).
- Technology decoupling has accelerated: export controls on advanced semiconductors (NVIDIA H100/H200/Blackwell to China) remain in force; TSMC is prohibited from manufacturing cutting-edge chips for Chinese customers.
- Taiwan tensions remain elevated but have not escalated into direct military confrontation. China has maintained regular military exercises near Taiwan. US Taiwan Relations Act commitments reaffirmed.
- Investment restrictions (CFIUS, outbound investment screening) are tightening; US-listed Chinese ADRs face ongoing delisting threats.
- CNY/USD at 6.92 — China is not weaponizing the yuan aggressively, preferring currency stability to attract foreign capital for its own recovery.
- The overall trajectory is managed competition, not hot war. Both sides have economic incentives to avoid further escalation but political incentives push toward continued friction.

### Russia–Ukraine

The war enters its fourth year with no peace settlement in sight, though the dynamics have shifted:

- Russia's GDP growth has slowed to +0.6% as the war economy strains (inflation: 5.9%, interest rate: 15%). Western sanctions and oil price caps are increasingly effective as enforcement improves.
- Ukraine's economy is showing surprising resilience (+2.8% GDP annual growth) supported by massive Western aid, but at the cost of fiscal deficits of -17.6% of GDP and debt/GDP of 89.8%.
- Ukraine's President Zelenskyy is actively courting Gulf state support, signing air defense deals with the UAE and Qatar.
- Trump administration has not delivered the "24-hour peace deal" promised in 2024; negotiations are stalled on territorial concessions (Ukraine refuses to cede occupied territory without security guarantees).
- European NATO members are surging defense spending. Germany committed to exceeding 2% of GDP; the European rearmament wave is creating a significant demand impulse for defense industrials.
- Energy implication: Russian natural gas is now essentially zero in European supply. European LNG imports have replaced most of this, making the Qatar LNG disruption a double shock for European energy security.

### US Domestic Policy (Trump 2.0)

Key policy developments shaping the investment landscape:

- **Tariffs:** Broad tariff regime in effect — 10% universal baseline tariff, 60%+ on China, sectoral tariffs on autos, steel, aluminum. Trading partners (EU, Canada, Mexico) have filed WTO complaints and implemented targeted counter-tariffs. The Jones Act was temporarily suspended (60 days) — an unusual signal of economic strain management.
- **DOGE / Spending Cuts:** The Department of Government Efficiency has been implementing significant federal workforce reductions. This is contributing to the non-farm payrolls weakness (government employment declining). The fiscal impact is estimated at $150–300bn in annualized savings, but the near-term economic drag is front-loaded.
- **Deregulation:** Financial services (bank capital rules eased, crypto regulatory clarity improving), energy (fast-tracking LNG terminals, offshore drilling), and AI (lighter-touch AI regulation vs. EU) are the headline beneficiary sectors. This partially offsets economic headwinds.
- **Fiscal outlook:** Despite DOGE cuts, the deficit remains >6% of GDP due to entitlement costs, defense spending, and interest expense (~$1.1 trillion/year on $36+ trillion debt at current rates). The 10Y yield staying above 4.4% reflects this fiscal risk premium.
- **"No Kings" protests:** Nationwide demonstrations on March 28 signal elevated domestic political tension, which is a latent risk for policy continuity.

### Europe

- **NATO and Defense:** Germany's new coalition government has passed a constitutional amendment to exclude defense spending from the debt brake, enabling a generational rearmament. Sweden and Finland fully integrated into NATO. Defense sector is one of Europe's best-performing equity segments in 2025–2026.
- **Elections:** France faces ongoing political instability (CAC -5.5% YTD partly reflects this). UK's Labour government is managing the economy relatively steadily; FTSE 100's sector composition (energy, financials, miners) is driving its +15% outperformance.
- **Energy Independence:** Europe has diversified away from Russian gas but remains vulnerable. The Qatar LNG disruption is a major setback. German energy costs are structurally higher than pre-2022 levels, representing a permanent competitive disadvantage for energy-intensive manufacturing.
- **ECB Flexibility:** With Eurozone inflation at 1.9% (at/below target), the ECB has room to cut further if the Iran shock triggers a European recession via energy prices. This is a potential H2 2026 catalyst for European fixed income.

## Key Risks for H2 2026

1. **Strait of Hormuz Closure / Full Iran War Escalation** (Probability: Medium-High): If Iran retaliates by mining or blockading the Strait of Hormuz, 20% of global energy supply is disrupted. WTI could spike to $130–160/barrel, triggering global recession, double-digit inflation, and equity markets -20 to -35% from current levels. Portfolio impact: devastating for equities/credit; beneficial for energy stocks, gold, short-duration bonds.

2. **US Stagflation / Fed Policy Error** (Probability: Medium): Core PCE at 3.1% and re-accelerating while payrolls turn negative puts the Fed in an impossible position. If the Fed cuts to address recession, inflation could re-spike (especially with oil at $100). If it holds, recession deepens. Either path is negative for growth assets. Portfolio impact: long bonds and equities both suffer; commodities, real assets, and TIPS benefit.

3. **China Property Sector Systemic Crisis** (Probability: Medium-Low): Property investment still falling -11% YoY; new home sales down -27%. If a major state-owned bank requires recapitalization or a broader credit event occurs, EM contagion could spread. Portfolio impact: EM equities and credit sell off sharply; commodity demand drops; AUD/commodity currencies weaken.

4. **US Recession (Hard Landing)** (Probability: Medium): February's -92K payrolls print, consumer confidence near record lows (53.3), and Q4 GDP at 0.7% annualized represent the precursors. A technical recession (two consecutive negative GDP quarters) in Q1–Q2 2026 would trigger earnings downgrades and multiple compression from already-elevated valuations. Portfolio impact: S&P 500 could fall to 5,000–5,500 (-15 to -22% from current).

5. **AI/Tech Bubble Correction** (Probability: Medium): Nasdaq already -8.4% YTD. If hyperscaler capex plans are revised (Microsoft, Google, Meta all spending $50–100bn+ on AI infrastructure), or if monetization timelines disappoint, the AI trade unwinds sharply. CAPE at 36.7x provides no cushion. Portfolio impact: Nasdaq -20 to -30%; S&P 500 more muted given sector weights; but momentum reversal could be rapid.

## Key Opportunities for H2 2026

1. **Energy Sector Equities and MLPs:** With WTI at $100/barrel and the supply disruption not fully priced in energy equities (which tend to lag spot), US E&P companies, integrated majors (Exxon, Chevron), and midstream MLPs offer earnings leverage to sustained high oil prices. If the Iran conflict continues, energy could be the best-performing sector for the second consecutive year.

2. **International Value Equities (UK, Japan, Select Europe):** FTSE 100 (+15% YTD) and Nikkei (+6% YTD) demonstrate the performance available from non-US equities trading at 10–14x forward earnings vs. US at 21–22x. UK large-caps (HSBC, BP, Shell, mining companies) combine low valuations with energy/commodity leverage and GBP optionality. Japanese corporate governance reforms continue to unlock shareholder value. Express via MSCI EAFE tilted toward UK/Japan.

3. **Gold and Precious Metals:** Despite the pullback from $5,608 ATH to $4,495, the structural drivers remain intact: dollar weakness, de-dollarization, negative or barely-positive real yields, and geopolitical risk. A re-test of the January ATH is plausible if Iran tensions persist or escalate. Gold mining equities (GDX/GDXJ) offer operating leverage to gold prices and are still trading at significant discounts to intrinsic NAV at current spot prices.

4. **Short-Duration / Inflation-Protected Fixed Income:** With the 2Y yield at 3.93% and the Fed on hold, short-duration investment grade credit (1–3 year) offers attractive yields with limited duration risk. TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) offer insurance against a re-acceleration of inflation if the oil shock passes through to CPI. Spread: real yields are positive but barely — making TIPS relatively attractive vs. nominal Treasuries.

5. **Defense and Infrastructure (US and Europe):** The geopolitical environment is structurally bullish for defense spending for years. European NATO rearmament is a multi-decade procurement cycle. US Tier 1 defense contractors (Lockheed, RTX, Northrop, L3Harris) are beneficiaries. Additionally, infrastructure spending (energy security, grid upgrades, onshoring supply chains) should compound regardless of the political cycle.
