## David Swensen

## My Challenge to George Soros: Reflexivity Is Not a Portfolio Strategy

George, I respect the intellectual framework — reflexivity is a genuine insight about how markets work. But there is a fundamental category error running through your entire proposal, and it is this: you have confused a macro narrative with an investment portfolio. Your 15% short-dollar position via FX forwards and options, your 10% S&P 500 put spreads, and your 5% oil call options combine to put 30% of this portfolio in derivatives structures with defined decay profiles, execution complexity, and timing dependency that are lethal to long-horizon return compounding. The Yale endowment does not run a global macro hedge fund. I manage a perpetual institution, not a trading book. Your portfolio requires being right not just directionally but temporally — the EUR/USD call at a 1.18 strike, the September 2026 S&P put spread, the WTI calls at $120-$130 strike — every one of these positions has an expiry date. When you are wrong on timing, you lose the premium and reconstruct the position at a higher cost. This is not a portfolio; it is a sequence of bets that must be re-underwritten every quarter. The expected value of that process, net of bid-ask spreads, option premiums, and the behavioral cost of rolling losing positions, is deeply negative for an institution like ours.

More specifically, your 25% gold allocation — the largest single position in your entire portfolio — is built on a thesis I find partially but not entirely compelling, and the structure matters enormously. GLD gives you spot exposure to gold at a 40 basis point annual fee. Fine. But GDX — your gold miners position within that 25% — is not gold. Gold mining equities have a notoriously poor long-run track record of capital allocation. Management teams at Barrick, Newmont, and the GDX basket have spent decades diluting shareholders through overpriced acquisitions at gold-price peaks, exactly when your thesis would have them flush with cash. The operating leverage that makes GDX attractive in a rising gold price environment works symmetrically on the downside — and it adds management risk, geopolitical risk (many mines are in jurisdictions with resource nationalism), and cost inflation risk that are entirely absent from physical gold. You are paying for complexity and getting unreliable leverage. If you believe in gold as a structural de-dollarization thesis — which I do find persuasive at a smaller allocation weight — you hold physical exposure or streaming royalty companies with predictable cost structures. You do not allocate 10% to miners and call it gold.

Finally, and I say this with genuine respect for your record: the 2026 version of the reflexive dollar-collapse thesis has been the dominant macro trade every year since 2003, and the dollar has confounded it repeatedly. DXY at 100 is not a crisis level — it is roughly where the dollar sat through most of 2014-2015 before surging back to 110+. The structural case for dollar weakness is real (fiscal deficit, political dysfunction, de-dollarization), but "structural" and "this year" are not the same claim. Your portfolio is built around the conviction that the reflexive loop breaks in H2 2026 specifically. I do not have that conviction. The institution I manage cannot afford to hold 15% in FX derivatives that require precise timing of a macro regime shift. The endowment's 30-year track record was built on structural asset class allocation, not on calling the dollar's breaking point in a six-month window.

## Defense of My Portfolio

The critique I anticipate from this committee — particularly from Buffett — is that my alternatives-heavy model requires manager access that creates an unfair comparison, that PE entry multiples at 10-13x EBITDA have compressed the illiquidity premium, and that the 2008-2009 liquidity crisis revealed a structural flaw in the endowment model. Let me address each directly. On manager access: yes, the Yale Model requires privileged access to top-quartile managers, and I have spent forty years building those relationships. That is a competitive advantage, not a hidden assumption. The solution for an institution that lacks those relationships is to acknowledge the constraint explicitly — not to abandon alternatives entirely, but to use secondaries markets (where we are buying existing LP positions at 8-15% discounts to reported NAV from motivated sellers, bypassing the access problem entirely) and direct co-investments alongside known managers. On valuations: PE at 10-13x EBITDA is genuinely elevated, and I said so explicitly in my Round 1 presentation. That is precisely why I am emphasizing secondaries as the primary entry vehicle — you acquire the illiquidity premium at a discount to current NAV rather than paying peak multiples as a new LP. On liquidity: the 2008-2009 experience taught me that the liquid buffer must be sized to cover 2-3 years of net capital calls in a stress scenario. I have incorporated that lesson. Buffett holding 35% in T-bills and calling it "rational" is not wrong for Berkshire Hathaway, a publicly-traded operating company with redemption obligations and a float to protect. It is the wrong framework for a perpetual endowment with a 5% annual spending distribution and no near-term liability matching requirement. Every dollar in T-bills is a toll on our long-run real return.

The other likely critique — from Dalio — is that my 0% conventional bond allocation is reckless in a scenario where equities and real assets sell off simultaneously in a deflationary hard-landing scenario. Ray, I have heard this critique for thirty years and I have a direct answer: the Yale endowment can tolerate the volatility of a simultaneous equity-and-real-asset drawdown because we have no forced selling mechanism. We are not an insurance company matching liabilities. We are not a pension fund with monthly benefit payments. We draw 5% annually from the endowment — a rate we can adjust at the margin — and the university has access to credit facilities for short-term operating needs. The scenario where bonds would save a perpetual institution is deflationary depression — and in that scenario, our real assets (timberland, infrastructure) generate biological or contractual cash flows that do not go to zero, while our PE portfolio holds businesses that produce goods and services people need regardless of financial market conditions. I will grant Ray one concession: a 5-8% allocation to short-duration TIPS as inflation protection is a legitimate addition in this specific macro environment, and I have incorporated that into my updated allocation below.
